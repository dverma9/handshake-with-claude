The Physical Laws
Principles 1–3: Squash & Stretch · Anticipation · Staging

Why Physics Matters in Animation

Animation is the art of controlled deception. Your audience has spent their entire life watching how the physical world behaves — gravity, mass, momentum, cause and effect. Their brain is a finely tuned physics engine. The moment something looks "wrong," the immersion breaks.

The first group of principles is rooted in the observable world: how objects deform under force, how bodies prepare for movement, and how a scene is arranged so the viewer knows exactly where to look. These are the bedrock principles — get them wrong and nothing else you do will save the animation.

📖 Historical Context
The 12 Principles were codified by Disney animators Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas in their 1981 book The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation. They emerged from decades of trial, error, and observation at Disney Studios during the golden age of animation (1930s–1960s).

Principle 1: Squash & Stretch

Squash and stretch is considered by many the single most important principle of animation. It gives the illusion of weight, mass, and flexibility. When a ball hits the ground, it squashes (compresses). When it bounces back up or flies through the air, it stretches (elongates). This deformation tells us how heavy, fast, and elastic the object is.

The golden rule: Volume must remain constant during squash and stretch. When a ball squashes horizontally, it must expand vertically to compensate. Violating this rule makes objects feel like they're magically gaining or losing mass.

With S&S ✓
Without S&S ✗
Notice how the left ball feels heavy and alive; the right feels like a rock.

Analogy: The Water Balloon

💡
Think of every animated object as a water balloon. When you press down on one, it doesn't compress — the water shifts sideways. The total volume stays the same, but the shape changes dramatically. A rubber ball is barely a water balloon. A jelly character is very much one. The elasticity of your squash and stretch communicates the material properties of your subject.

Squash & Stretch Applied to Characters

This principle isn't just for balls! When a character is surprised, their eyes widen (stretch). When they land from a jump, their whole body flattens (squash). When running fast, limbs stretch in the direction of motion. Even subtle facial expressions rely on this: raised eyebrows stretch the forehead, puffed cheeks squash the face.

Diagram — Volume Conservation in Squash & Stretch
⚠ Common Misconception

"Squash and stretch only applies to cartoon animation — it would look silly in realistic 3D."

✅ Reality: Squash and stretch exists in all great 3D animation, including Pixar's hyper-realistic work. It's often subtle — a cheek puff, a slight body compress on landing — but it's always there. Without it, even photorealistic characters feel like plastic mannequins. The amount of exaggeration is what varies, not the principle itself.

Principle 2: Anticipation

Anticipation is a preparatory movement that occurs before the main action. Before a character punches, they pull their arm back. Before a jump, they crouch. Before throwing a ball, they wind up. This prepares the audience for what's about to happen, making the subsequent action feel powerful, deliberate, and believable.

Without anticipation, actions feel abrupt and jarring — like someone editing the first frame of a video. With it, they feel natural and grounded in physical reality. Anticipation also applies in reverse: a character can pause or "settle" after a big action (this becomes "follow-through," covered in Module 3).

DEMO — Anticipation Before Action
Watch the ball pull back (anticipation) before launching forward. Without the pull-back, the launch would feel random.

The Three Functions of Anticipation

FunctionExampleEffect
Signals intentionA character looks left before running leftAudience knows what's coming and can follow
Builds energyArm pulling back before a punchMakes the main action feel more powerful
Adds weightBody sinking before jumpingCommunicates mass and effort required

Analogy: The Bowler

🎳
A bowler doesn't just instantly release the ball — they swing their arm back first. That backswing is pure anticipation. Its duration and depth tell you how hard they're about to throw. A tiny backswing = gentle roll. A huge backswing = power throw. The anticipation sets the expectation for what follows.

Principle 3: Staging

Staging is the art of presenting an idea clearly. It's borrowed directly from theatre and film: where do you position the character? What's in the background? What angle does the camera take? The goal is that the audience instantly understands what is happening, without ambiguity.

Great staging means that if you took a single frame from the middle of an action, a person who had never seen the film before could understand what's happening. Poor staging clutters the scene, buries the key action, or presents it from an angle where the key motion is hidden.

Silhouette Test

The fastest way to evaluate staging is the silhouette test: fill your character with solid black and see if the pose is still readable. If it's not — if limbs blend together, if the action is unclear — the staging needs work.

Diagram — Good vs Bad Staging (Silhouette Test)
Staging vs. Composition
Staging encompasses composition but goes further. Composition is a static art concept (where things are placed). Staging in animation also includes timing, camera movement, and how elements are revealed over time. A well-staged scene uses all of these to direct the viewer's attention at exactly the right moment.

🔑 Module 1 Key Takeaways
  • Squash & stretch communicates weight, elasticity, and life — volume must always be conserved
  • Anticipation prepares the viewer for action and makes subsequent movement feel powerful
  • Staging is about clarity — if the viewer doesn't immediately understand what's happening, it fails
  • These three principles are the most grounded in physics and theatre — they make scenes feel real
  • Apply the silhouette test to any pose: if it's readable as a black shape, it's well staged

Principles 4–6: Straight-Ahead vs Pose-to-Pose · Follow Through & Overlapping Action · Slow In & Slow Out

How Animators Actually Work

The principles in this module address the process and mechanics of creating animation — not just the physics, but the method. How do you approach a complex scene? How do you handle multiple objects moving at once? How do you make motion feel organic rather than mechanical? These principles answer those questions.

Principle 4: Straight-Ahead vs Pose-to-Pose

These are two distinct methods of creating animation, and each has different strengths. Great animators know when to use which approach — and many combine them.

Straight-Ahead
Draw or key frame by frame, from beginning to end. Natural, spontaneous, fluid — but hard to control timing or maintain proportions. Best for: fire, water, smoke, wild action sequences.
✓ Organic feel   ✓ Spontaneous   ✗ Hard to control
Pose-to-Pose
Plan the key poses first (start, middle, end), then fill in the "in-between" frames. Precise, controllable, structured. Best for: dialogue, dramatic moments, precise timing in performance.
✓ Precise timing   ✓ Controlled   ✗ Can feel stiff
🎬
Modern digital animation largely uses pose-to-pose (setting keyframes), but great animators introduce straight-ahead-style spontaneity by using graph editors to sculpt the motion curves between keys — blending the precision of one method with the fluidity of the other.

Principle 5: Follow-Through & Overlapping Action

These two closely related principles describe what happens after the main action stops and how different parts of a body move at different rates.

Follow-Through

When a character stops, not all parts of them stop at the same time. A running dog stops its body, but its ears, tail, and fur continue moving forward — then settle back. A woman stops walking; her ponytail continues swinging. This is follow-through: parts that are lighter or more flexible lag behind and overshoot the stopping point before coming to rest.

Overlapping Action

While follow-through deals with what happens after stopping, overlapping action deals with different body parts moving at different times and speeds during a movement. When a character turns their head, the neck leads, then the head follows, then the hair lags behind. Nothing moves as one rigid unit — different elements start and end at different times.

With overlap (tail lags)
No overlap (rigid)
The small dot (a "tail") lags behind the main object on the left — this is overlapping action in action.

Analogy: The Orchestra

🎻
A symphony orchestra doesn't have every instrument play the same note at the same instant. Different sections enter and exit, overlap and follow each other. If they all played in perfect unison all the time, it would sound robotic. Animation works the same way: when every body part moves in lockstep, the character feels like a robot. Stagger the timing and suddenly there's life.

Principle 6: Slow In & Slow Out (Easing)

This is arguably the principle that separates beginner animation from professional work. In the real world, nothing starts or stops instantaneously. A car doesn't go from 0 to 60 in one frame. A hand doesn't move at constant speed from point A to point B — it accelerates at first, then decelerates at the end.

"Slow in" means more frames near the end of a movement (deceleration). "Slow out" means more frames near the beginning (acceleration). In modern software this is called easing, and it's controlled by curves in a graph editor.

INTERACTIVE — Compare Easing Types
Ease In-Out (natural)
Linear (robotic)
Ease In (starts slow)
Ease Out (ends slow)
Notice how ease-in-out feels natural, while linear feels mechanical and cheap.

When to Break the Rules

Linear motion (no easing) isn't always wrong. Machines, robots, and supernatural elements can intentionally use linear motion to feel inhuman or mechanical. The power of knowing the rule is knowing when breaking it is the right artistic choice.

⚠ Common Misconception

"Slow in and slow out means the animation is slow overall."

✅ Reality: The total duration of the animation can be anything. "Slow in" and "slow out" refer to the distribution of frames within that duration — more frames clustered at the start/end, fewer in the middle. A fast, snappy 12-frame animation can still use slow in/out; it just means 3–4 frames at the extremes and a fast blur in the middle.

🔑 Module 2 Key Takeaways
  • Straight-ahead creates organic fluid motion; pose-to-pose creates precise controlled performance
  • Follow-through: parts of a character continue moving after the main action stops
  • Overlapping action: different body parts start and stop at different times — nothing moves as one rigid unit

  • Linear motion = mechanical, robotic — use it intentionally, not by default

Motion Quality
Principles 7–9: Arcs · Secondary Action · Timing

The Invisible Craft

The principles in this module are often invisible when done correctly — and glaringly obvious when violated. Arcs, secondary action, and timing are what make an animation feel effortless and natural. They operate below the level of conscious perception for most viewers, but their absence is immediately felt as something being "off."

Principle 7: Arcs

Every natural movement in the world follows a curved path, not a straight one. This is a physical reality: momentum, gravity, and the rotational nature of limbs all conspire to create curved trajectories. A hand swinging from the shoulder describes an arc. A thrown ball follows a parabola. Eyes moving from left to right trace a slight curve.

The most common beginner mistake is creating "twinning" — straight-line motion between two points. This feels mechanical and dead. Introduce even a subtle arc and the motion immediately comes alive.

DEMO — Arc vs Straight-Line Motion
Arc path ✓ Natural
Straight path ✗ Mechanical
The ball on the left traces a natural arc; the right slides mechanically in a straight line.

Arcs in Character Animation

The arc principle extends far beyond throwing and jumping. Even the motion of a character's eyes, the movement of fingers on a piano, and the turn of a head should all follow curved paths. The straighter the path, the more robotic and computer-generated it feels — ironically, since computers default to linear interpolation, animators must manually impose arcs.

Diagram — How Arcs Arise from Rotational Limb Movement

Principle 8: Secondary Action

Secondary actions are supporting movements that reinforce or add depth to the main action. They must never compete with or distract from the primary action — instead, they enrich it and add dimensionality to the character.

A character walking (primary action) might swing their arms, move their hair in the breeze, adjust their bag, or blink — these are secondary actions. Together they create the sense of a fully alive being rather than a puppet executing a single command.

Primary ActionGood Secondary ActionWhy it Works
Character runningArms pumping, slight body lean, face showing effortAdds exertion and personality without competing
Character speakingSubtle eyebrow raises, hand gestures, weight shiftsMakes speech feel embodied, not disembodied
Character sleepingGentle chest rise, occasional twitching, eyelids flutterMakes stillness feel alive, not frozen
Ball bouncingSubtle rotation as it bouncesAdds realism — balls spin in the real world
⚠ The Secondary Action Trap
Secondary action becomes a problem when it overwhelms the primary. If a character is delivering emotional dialogue and their secondary actions (fidgeting, gesturing wildly) are more interesting than their face and words, the composition has broken. Secondary actions must be subordinate — they support, never compete.

Principle 9: Timing

Timing is the principle that determines how many frames an action takes. In traditional animation at 24fps: a 12-frame action happens in half a second, a 24-frame action in a full second. Timing communicates weight, mood, and personality more powerfully than almost any other principle.

A heavy boulder rolling: slow timing (many frames). A hummingbird wing: fast timing (few frames, sometimes a blur). A sad character walking: slow, heavy timing. An excited child running: fast, bouncy timing. The same action — a hand reaching for a cup — feels completely different with slow, deliberate timing vs fast, nervous timing.

Timing as Storytelling

🕐
Consider a comedic "take" — a character sees something shocking. If the take happens in 2 frames, it's barely noticeable. In 8 frames, it reads. In 4 frames with a hold and then a slow settle, it's comedic gold. Timing IS the joke in comedy animation. The same is true for drama — a 3-second pause before a character speaks can be more emotionally devastating than any line of dialogue.

Spacing and Timing

Timing (number of frames) works together with spacing (how far apart the positions are between frames). Fast action = large spacing between frames. Slow action = small spacing. Easing = variable spacing. Understanding the relationship between timing and spacing is what separates the professionals.

Diagram — How Timing + Spacing Create Different Feels
🔑 Module 3 Key Takeaways
  • Arcs: nearly all natural motion follows curved paths — straight-line motion feels robotic and digital
  • Secondary action enriches the primary action without competing with it — it must always be subordinate
  • Timing (number of frames) communicates weight, personality, and mood more powerfully than almost any other principle
  • Spacing and timing work together — understanding their relationship is the mark of a professional animator
  • Slow timing = heavy, deliberate, dramatic. Fast timing = light, energetic, comedic or nervous

Art & Appeal
Principles 10–12: Exaggeration · Solid Drawing · Appeal

The Artistic Summit

The final three principles move from physics and process into the realm of pure artistic sensibility. These are the principles that separate technically competent animation from truly memorable, emotionally resonant work. They require taste, observation, and a deep understanding of human perception — things that can be learned but not automated.

Principle 10: Exaggeration

Exaggeration is the principle that animation isn't about perfect realism — it's about capturing the essence of reality and amplifying it. A surprised character doesn't just open their eyes slightly wider; their eyes bug out of their head. A sneaky character doesn't just lean forward slightly; they hunch, tiptoe, and peek with cartoonish discretion. This is not about being unrealistic — it's about being more real than real.

Walt Disney said: "Take a real action and push it to the extreme." The trap beginners fall into is "too subtle" — they animate something realistic and wonder why it looks dead on screen. The camera reduces everything; you need to push further than feels comfortable in life.

Exaggeration is Relative

😐
Too subtle
Realistic but dead on screen. Audiences don't feel it.
😲
Well exaggerated
Amplified truth. The emotion reads clearly and immediately.
🤪
Over-exaggerated
Grotesque or incoherent. Loses meaning entirely.
🎨
Exaggeration requires understanding what the essence of an action is, then amplifying that essence while preserving everything else. When exaggerating fear, you push the eyes wider, the shoulders up, the body backward — all in service of the core emotional truth. Random exaggeration that amplifies the wrong thing creates confusion, not emphasis.
⚠ Common Misconception

"Exaggeration is only for cartoon or comedy animation — realistic animations shouldn't use it."

✅ Reality: Every effective animation — including realistic drama — uses exaggeration. The degree varies: a realistic drama might exaggerate by 10–15%, a cartoon by 200%. Without any exaggeration, even realistic animation looks like a motion-captured actor performing with no direction. Exaggeration is what transforms motion capture data into performance.

Principle 11: Solid Drawing

Solid drawing is the principle that animated characters must feel three-dimensional and have a sense of weight, depth, and form — even if they're drawn in 2D. This comes from an understanding of anatomy, perspective, and volume, and extends into 3D animation as understanding how to pose characters convincingly in three-dimensional space.

The Three-Dimensional Illusion

A flat, unconvincing character "floats" on the screen — it doesn't feel like it inhabits the same space as its environment. A solidly drawn character feels present, grounded, and tangible. This requires animators to constantly think about where the weight is, how the body is supported, and what the camera's perspective does to the reading of a pose.

Solid Drawing PrincipleWhat to DoCommon Violation
Volume consistencyCharacter's head stays the same size as they turnHead grows or shrinks when seen from different angles
Weight distributionBalance point is always under the center of massCharacter appears to float or tip without support
Twinning avoidanceBoth arms/legs never do the exact same thing at onceBoth arms raised to exact same height simultaneously
Depth and perspectiveOverlapping forms establish what's in front/behindFlat designs where depth is unclear

Twinning: The Silent Killer

Twinning occurs when both sides of a character mirror each other exactly — same arm angles, same leg positions, same eye direction. It instantly makes characters feel stiff and robotic. Nature abhors perfect symmetry in motion, and so does good animation. Always offset and vary the two sides of a character.

Principle 12: Appeal

Appeal is the most subjective principle, and perhaps the most important. A character with appeal has charisma — a quality that makes the audience want to watch them. This doesn't mean every character must be cute or likeable. A menacing villain has appeal if they're compelling. A tragic character has appeal if they're emotionally resonant.

Appeal in design comes from clarity of shape, readable silhouette, and a design that's simple enough to be understood at a glance but rich enough to reward further attention. Appeal in animation comes from the same source as great acting: specificity, commitment, and the sense that every movement comes from a genuine internal motivation.

The Elements of Appeal

Shape Clarity
Simple, readable shapes that can be understood instantly. Disney characters are built from basic geometric forms — circles, triangles, rectangles — that convey personality at a glance.
Design
Specificity
Characters that feel like individuals, not types. The way THIS character scratches their nose is different from any other character — small details of behavior create uniqueness.
Performance
Asymmetry
Slightly asymmetric designs feel more natural and alive. Perfect symmetry reads as mask-like and artificial. A subtle tilt, a slightly different eye size, creates organic appeal.
Design
Commitment
Characters with appeal commit fully to their emotions and actions. Halfway expressions or ambivalent poses read as weak. Go all in on the emotional beat.
Performance

🔑 Module 4 Key Takeaways
  • Exaggeration amplifies the essence of an action — too subtle is as much of a failure as too exaggerated
  • Solid drawing gives characters volume, weight, and a tangible presence in their environment
  • Twinning (both sides mirroring perfectly) makes characters feel robotic — always vary and offset

✅ Twinning! When both sides of a character mirror each other perfectly, the result looks stiff and robotic. Nature and organic movement are never perfectly symmetrical. Animators deliberately offset limb positions, vary arm angles, and stagger movements to avoid twinning.

🎬 Project Brief: The Character Bounce
Create a short animation (3–8 seconds) of a character with a distinct personality bouncing a ball. This deceptively simple premise requires you to apply all 12 principles simultaneously — making it the definitive test of your understanding.
  • 1Design your character — choose a personality (nervous, excitable, sluggish, pompous). Use shape language from the Appeal principle to communicate this in their silhouette.
  • 2Plan your staging — choose an angle where both the character AND the ball are clearly visible and readable. Run the silhouette test on your key poses.
  • 3Animate the ball — apply squash & stretch, timing, arcs, and slow in/out to the ball itself before adding the character.
  • 4Add the character's action — show anticipation before they throw/catch. What secondary actions reveal their personality? Do their clothes/hair show follow-through?
  • 5Exaggerate deliberately — take your first pass and push it 20% further. Does it read better? Keep pushing until you've gone "too far," then pull back slightly.
  • 6Check all 12 — go through the cheat sheet and verify each principle is present. Note any that are missing and add them.
